## I. A Phenomenology of Suffering 

Phenomenological Ethics begins with the two-fold emphasis of:

#1a. proposing a ‘scientific’ description

#1b. that attends to ethical and normative conditions of whatever situation or thing that presents itself in one’s account-giving (to another in our common lived world)

#2a. the description proceeds from whatever scientific language is most readily available and appropriate for the description at hand, enacted with self-conscious awareness that this description is done by me, as an individual subject limited to the spatio-temporal perspective that is my very own as I am situated with this body and a particular place, at a specific time, and with a particular history and soci-cultural, political, and economic context

#2b. the attention to ethical and normative conditions is directed by the body of moral principles and/or values that systematically determine the structures within which humans find themselves; and the ‘moral’ behavior which an individual human uses to evaluate/discriminate between choices that presents the possibilities for a judgment that leads to a choice and, ultimately, to an embodied action

#3. The starting point is the traditional starting point of phenomenologists, namely, the natural givenness in which I find myself, conditioned by my unique familial heritage, genetics, languages and learned behaviors and the professional and learned languages and behaviors of whatever scientific field I have been trained in. For instance, it could be that I am Anglo-American male, have come from a working-class background, been educated in a typical U.S. American regional school system in the middle of the twentieth century, received a fundamental and comprehensive liberal education that included solid grounding in the STEM fields. It may also be the case that I am trained in the language and methodologies of the upper-tier of U.S. American and European universities, having attained a PhD in this or that field, in my case, in philosophy. In that case, in my particular subjective case, my initial ‘natural’ perspective will be determined by those conditions. Ethically, my description is conditioned by my Christian upbringing, my disaffection with religion as a teenager and through being exposed to philosophy and existentialism, my inter-religious development as a young adult and training in Jewish philosophy and ethics (divine commandment theory), and finally, my many years of teaching ethical theory (Virtue Ethics, Deontological Ethics, Utilitarian Ethics), and the socio-political economic system in which I find myself. In my case, that would be late Capitalist social-contract theory liberalism, influenced by Marxist/Communist revolutionary adjustments that established broad protections and benefits for the working class, such as a nationally mandated minimum wage, universal health care (attenuated), social security, OSHA laws (worker safety), and in some cases the right to bargain and strike if working conditions are unacceptable. 

#4. I BRACKET my particular, subjective conditions—but with the conscious awareness of my particular conditions and, thus, conscious awareness of my existing biases (to the best of my ability to be aware of these). I BRACKET my conditions to provide a description of this or that situation with the language and the training that is naturally mine at this particular place and time in my life. 

#5. What does it mean to BRACKET? What is Husserl’s EPOCHÉ?
The FIRST REDUCTION:

Technically, involves suspending or holding off the status of the ‘real’ existence of the ‘intended’ object at hand as well as questions having to do with the object’s physical or actual characteristics. For example, seeing a table is a conscious, cognitive experience whether the table happens to be in the ‘real’ world, in my dream, or occurs as a hallucination. I bracket ‘table’ by suspending judgment about what the table is in itself to be able to analyze how the occurrence of ‘table’ as a phenomenon that appears at a specific moment in my existence happens to be appearing in my particular, subjective consciousness. 

In bracketing, I become attentive to my given, existing conditions, contextual implications, habitual biases, and scientific training and parameters, and intellectual or cultural commitments. I give an oral or written account—a description—of the situation at hand, with the full awareness that such a description is MY perspective on the given state of affairs or the object of my intention and attention. That perspective proceeds from me as that sort of being that is capable of providing more than one perspective on any given state of affairs of any objective thing. For instance, I could walk around the thing and provide a description of the thing from any number of spatial temporal points—the front, the sides, the top, the bottom, the back. I could wait until later in the day and provide a description of the thing or situation when the sun is setting and the light is fading, and would paint the state of affairs in whatever colors were at my disposal then and there, even gray on gray. To the best of my ability, I focus on the object or thing or state of affairs at hand, and describe whatever I see with the language that I have at my disposal and with as much thoroughness and completeness as possible, given my linguistic abilities, the prosaic language and technical vocabulary most familiar and ‘natural’ to me. And, finally, I hold, isolate, or highlight the thing or state of affairs ‘in itself’ and ‘at a distance’ from its connectedness to its background and context. At the same time, I keep in conscious awareness the fact that I am BRACKETING and intentionally holding at a distance those connections which, while held in abeyance, are nonetheless still there and thus operative in my descriptive interpretation. 

The tentative goal or the crux of phenomenological activity is to be able to analyze or ‘unpack’ the phenomenon of ‘table’ by systematically ‘seeing’ and becoming aware of the operative functions of different layers of symbolic meanings that are ‘attached to’ or ‘associated with’ the particular bracketed phenomenon. In this way, I am able to arrive at the essential or purity of what is meant and experienced by this phenomenon. I arrive at its essential characteristics that constitute its objectivity and thus, its temporal and spatial identity and the origin of its meaningful communicability. 

#6. ‘ETHICAL’ BRACKETING:  

The awareness with which I remain consciously attentive of those biases and suspended connections, even while not prioritizing them, is not merely an epistemological or even just an ontological stance, although it is both of those as well. Rather, that sort of awareness already entails an ethical awareness because of the normative status of the relational condition. What that means is that in so far as I become aware of the presuppositions involved in the intentional relationship of myself to this or that object or state of affairs I become aware of the quality of my motivations—desires, needs, interests—in that specific intentional relationship. This sort of awareness opens up the ‘ethical’ quality of the phenomenological ethics.

#7. SECOND REDUCTION:

Even given the fact that I am able to come up with a description of an object that is true to my particular, subjective experience of that object that description nonetheless is conditioned and limited by my initial ‘natural’ presuppositions, biases, and initial conditions. The limitation and inadequate objectivity, while disclosed essential core characteristic of the identity of the object as it endures over time and space, none the less in itself calls for and is open to ongoing explications because of the essential non-fixity and non-fixable nature of any and every object. The thing itself is subject to an infinite variety of perspectives and, thus, calls for an ‘other’ perspective that is not mine. This becomes apparent what is at stake in my relationship to the thing as one thing among many in a lived world that is ongoingly constituted intersubjectively. I live and find meaning and make meaning in a world with other subjects like myself but absolutely—as independent subjects—other than myself. Thus, to be true to the object, in my description of the object I allow my for the possibility that not only could my initial description of the object be limited, inadequate or complete but it could in fact be entirely wrong. The table could appear to me as a hallucination when, with the assistance of another perspective, the evidence that is provided to me convinces me—leads me to believe and thus accept—that the table exists as actual and in the ‘real’ and naturally objective world. 
The Second Reduction entails calling into question my initial ‘natural’ description and modifying that with the perspective of a radical and absolute other subjective who is not my alter ego. In this instance, I would be taught taught something new (about the thing or state of affairs) by that other subjective and with that other perspective. 

#8. the SECOND ETHICAL REDUCTION

The ethicality involved in the second reduction is such that I become aware of the normative power and potential determinative influences of my initial conditions. I become conscious of and able to recognize the presuppositions and biases and scientific framework and metrics that I realized were at work in my first reduction. At this point, ethical learning occurs.  This is what Levinas refers to as the “curve of intersubjectivty.” I become self-reflectively aware of and attentive to the other whose perspective calls mine into question and the validity and legitimacy of their own essential, core and pure subjective intentionalities—with respect to the object or state of affairs. The ‘curve’ occurs and throws into question the naivete of my ‘natural’ starting point with its biases and presuppositions and its only at this point that discourse commences, optimization begins, and that we can begin to talk about the differences in our subjective intentional relationships to the object and in our intersubjective relationship itself, with each other and in the lived world that we co-inhabit. 

#9. the THIRD REDUCTION

Happens when the perspective of the THIRD, who stands outside the intersubjective relationship, is introduced. This becomes the basis of establishing justice and objective, worldly institutions such as schools, courts, health-care facilities, and governments. 

#10. INTUITION

All along, and at every level of relationship, the nature of intuition changes as well. Intentionality and intuition go hand in hand as co-constitutive or our intentional and non-intentional experiences in our intersubjectively lived world(s).  Intuition guides our intentional relationship and every attempt at descriptive and analytic reduction. My intuition proceeds from my previous experiences of this or that object or state of affairs and leads, as it were, my expectations and search for evidence. The discovery of evidence that corroborates my intuitive grasp of this or that thing, leads to an elevation of my confidence and trust in the conclusions that I draw from my conscious, intellectual and emotional activity. The same holds true for the course of the second and third reductions. 

#11. Given that phenomenological ethics occurs as subjective, the practice of phenomenological ethics happens as an embodied experience (cf, especially Stein, Merleau-Ponty, and Levinas).

#12. Given that my concern is for examining my own presuppositions and biases, and accepting those as mine—because they constitute an essential, grounding dimension of my ownmost subjectivity (my ipseity, to speak with Levinas), I also become aware of and respect the presuppositions and biases of the subjectivity of the other as absolutely other than me. 

#13. I come into contact with the other through a combination of physical/sensual needs and desires AND through the metaphysical desire of goodness, of coming to know and desire the other as, ultimately, infinitely unknowable in essence, and thus—in a good sense, desire feeds desire in a spiraling relationship (back and forth, of activity and passivity) of responsibility. This is not based on relationship of mutual, dialectical reaction or even recognition, but is based on establishing an asymmetrical relationship of speaking-listening that allows for the other to express herself/himself from their own place, in their own time and from the absolute otherness of their subjectivity.

In this way, phenomenological ethics is essentially an ethics of responsibility, even more than compassion, sympathy, or empathy, phenomenological ethics is an ethics of infinite responsibility that relies on cultivating and nourishing the multiplicity and plurality of perspectives, voices, and questioning and affirming subjects, acting towards accomplishing and being nourished from more fruitful forms of communication and flourishing fecundity. 

In this way, the presupposition of peace precedes the dialectics of war and remains and is sustained by the ongoing face-to-face and personal practices of phenomenological ethics.  

#14. Phenomenological ethics precedes political analysis and political conditions and is anarchic in the sense that practicing this approach or method questions axiomatic and dogmatic founding principles of any and every political system. It is a method of ongoing and embodied critique, both self-, and other-critique, and prioritizes, motivates, and cultivates the confluence of ever-heightened levels (stages?) of awareness of a radical form of responsibility.
