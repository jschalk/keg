Language for Appiah is a method by which he advances his ethical framework.

Kwame Appiah quotes Sense and Sensibility by Jane Austen at the beginning of Chapter 3. He links that quote to an attack on the principle that we can trust our intuitions however he is such a talented writer that he could have included the quote simply because he wanted to share a part of one of his favorite novels. If I were to attempt the same I would quote Dune by Frank Herbert: 

> “I remember your gom jabbar,” Paul said. “You remember mine. I can kill you with a word.” The Fremen around the hall glanced knowingly at each other. Did the legend not say: “And his word shall carry death eternal to those who stand against righteousness.” [Italics not mine]

In the book Paul does is narratively given the supernatural ability to kill people by his breath and vocal cords. This fictional power is not really so far from the powers of depots who can decide to kill whomever they think should die. This power is justified by the despot’s claim to moral righteousness. But Despotism as historical fact has diminished: that power to righteously kill is now (somewhat mostly) in the hands of laws built by societies and subjected to intense communication and discussion. Where then did the flow of moral righteousness, once powerful enough to give a single human despotic power over entire populations, revert its waters? Into moral language! The modern powers of government cannot exist without a language justification from those who exercise power to those grant the power. Kwame Appiah’s book may seem more timid than my talk of despotism but in the actual world with actual people morality and moral language have awesome effects on what people actually do.

In this situation the language used for authority is clearly public. However Appiah makes a stronger claim than this that is foundational to ethical living: all moral language has a sameness. In the prologue of Experiments in Ethics Appiah says “...moral language is, indeed, language-something public, social and open for inspection.” (p 25) I best understand Kwame Anthony’s insistence that moral language is public language as an argument against isolated moral systems and towards a more socially responsive system. Where Appiah wants to take the reader from lonely isolated ethics to a an ethics unbreakable with social interaction.

To support this contention I discuss the isolated language with regards to the Virtue Ethics rejected in Chapter 2 ‘The Case against Character’ and the rejected again in simple intuitionism of Chapter 3 ‘The Case against Intuition’. In both cases the isolated internal analysis is rejected as inadequate. Then the importance of open language in contrast to isolated language will be analyzed with respect to the Appiah’s critique of the philosophical community. Lastly I will discuss the importance of data in fashioning supporting moral language as open for inspection. 

### How do you use language to discuss language?

The claim that “...moral language is, indeed, language-something public, social, and open for inspection” was initially obtuse and difficult for me. There is always been for me something strange about discussing the philosophy of language using language. It seems correct sentences concerning language should be able to look back on themselves somehow. It seems an impossible hurdle to for sentences to prove themselves. Perhaps there is a philosophy of sentences that would help me gain clarity.

However once serious consideration of the three words public, social, open began the pervasiveness Appiah’s moral language framework surfaced throughout the entire book. ‘Public’ meant that it must be findable for anyone and not a hidden secret. ‘Social’ meant that it had to immersed in humanity, touching any conversation with any person and not distance: no prophets. And maybe most importantly ‘Open’ meant that the moral language must be inviting and accepting of questioning and reluctant and defensive. Once what moral language was not had been defined much of the purpose of Appiah’s moral project became more clear. We need a toolbox full of every good tool we can get but also need to know that we are working in everyone’s space. This kind of methodological pluralism is does not make language simple. “The messiness of ethics goes down deep.” (p 201) 

### Virtue Ethics and Moral Language

Aristotle and Virtue Ethics are important for Appiah. Aristotle is the first reference he mentions “...what it is for a human life to go well. That was one of Aristotle’s central insights.” (p 1) and the eudaimonia is the last concept he references “The end [purpose] of philosophical ethics is to make sense of the project of eudaimonia.” (p 203) Those two are referred to, analyzed, and commented on repeatedly throughout the entire book. What I want to concentrate on is how language is used to present his case against internal-only Virtue Ethics.

The open inspection of language becomes the main tool with which he launches a cripplingly attack on “The power core of virtue ethics... [as] ...the concept of the virtuous person.” (p 34) He argues that people in the actual world sometimes act virtuously and sometimes don’t. If judged on a whole even never changing fictional characters have some traits that are virtuous and some are not. The Virtue Ethicist (we shall call her ‘she’) is not unprepared for this attack. She can argue the concept that all traits are as best as they could be per the virtue core. In response to that Appiah says that “...traits aren’t served up in a fixed combination like a characterological Happy Meal.” (p 39) (This Happy Meal analysis is funny and deep as a Happy Meal is a product of millions of dollars of economic utilitarian analysis. A Happy Meal is what a corporation does to get your money.) But cleverness aside the ethicist still has her argument if she says that Virtue is innately the reason people do what they do. Here Appiah forces a choice between purely internal language and justification and external presence in the public world by presenting study after study that situations matter. Appiah concludes from these studies the following:

> “...empirical moral psychology can help us think about how to manage our lives, how to become better people. Situationism rightly directs us to focus on institutions, on creating circumstances that are conducive to virtue. Virtue theorists have sometimes directed us toward an excessively inward model of self-development; situationism returns us to the world in which our selves take shape.” (p 71)
  
This leap (for the Virtue Ethicist) is impossible if she refuses the validity of empirical data in moral language. So Appiah’s ethical project fails its first challenge if empirical evidence goes out of bounds of a pretend moral language that is private and insulated. 

Often a ethicist or a small ethical community generates their moral claims from a reading or oral history and then applies that framework to the world.  They use a private language that puts the empirical world into its categories instead of interacting with opposing views. For example in general economic consumption theory a person is ‘utility-maximizer’ and as such is completely alone where the other is only a trading partner. The only question for the person is how to improve themself. The only person to answer the question is themself too. If moral language is allowed to be private instead of leaving it open to public inspection Appiah’s purpose is much harder if not impossible.

The eudaimonia project (beyond the scope of this paper) of the book also requires moral language. The simple translation of eudaimonia as internal happiness is rejected and instead it is necessary, as Appiah writes: 

> “To see each other person not as someone with preferences, pleasures, and pains, but as a creature engaged in the project of making a life, striving to succeed on the basis of standards that are partly found and partly made.” (p 203) 

A basis for succeeding in life can only be partly made within a moral language. It must be public to all and open to inspection otherwise there are others, engaged in the project, who are being excluded. This is a reason why moral saints Appiah chooses are so important: “Budda, Christ, Mother Teresa” (p 49) spoke in universal terms and as public figures are open to debate and thus part of general society. Contrast that with Muhammah, considered a Saintly figure by a significant portion of humanity, who didn’t make the list.

What we should take away from all this is that moral language not only is public, social and open to inspection but must be. 

### Intuition and Moral Language

The previously mentioned Jane Austen quote supported the absolute role of intuition in moral decision making. Appiah shows that every intuitionist is really, when actually justifying themself, not only an intuitionist. Others are needed. Public language is needed. When the language is opened good things can happen. The toolbox expands but still includes intuition. 

Appiah quotes Thomas Reid “...founder of the so-called Common Sense school...”take for granted, as first principles, things wherein we find an universal agreement”...” (p 74) How does Thomas Reid justify his knowledge of universal agreement to others who claim to not agree? Maybe he tells them that they don’t understand what they have universally agreed to!

Not only is there the analytical problem with the structure of the common sense argument there are historical examples of common sense being repulsive. Individuals born outside the power structure in America and other countries were/are oppressed and this oppression was/is often supported by ‘common sense’. It is common sense in America today that hard drugs are a criminal problem. Compared to Portugal’s approach of treating hard drugs as a medical problem the American system is barbaric. If the moral language of the United States was as open as Appiah wishes it to be than perhaps the ‘common sense’ drug policy would change.

Appiah contends that intuition is active in modern moral philosophy but that it is subject to public moral language. In particular he mentions John Rawls and the theory of reflective equilibrium. I don’t really understand reflective equilibrium (I will be take a course on it next semester) but Appiah mentions that Rawls “made explicit reference to scientific theorizing” (p 75) Scientific language is public, social and open to inspection. Since Rawls is a moral philosopher then the language used for this intuitionist is precisely the moral language Appiah is interested in. Later in the chapter he uses that openness to inspection to attack reflective equilibrium as “...stipple with appeals to shared intuitions...what “we are confident about”...What do you mean “we,” Kemo Sabe?” (p 80) Nothing wrong with using a theory’s own tools to show that that theory needs better tools. 

The experiments in Trolleyology, conceptualizes of emergencies with narrator-decided parameters, represent an isolated language. How open to inspection can a question of whether to kill one or kill five others be when the narrator decides all the circumstances? In the actual world there must be a language that is public, social, and open to inspection to best know what is even going on in a moral emergency. If I imagine not being in the same moral community as the persons who tell me what a trolley is, who I experience the trolley with, how would I be able to even know how a trolley functions and how it would be broken.

Appiah doesn’t reject intuition he just contends that it needs it to be directly connected to things in the world. He quotes John Stuart Mill to contrast with the anti-intuitionist with their intuitionist claims embedded in their theories.

> “Mill assumes that the desire to hurt those who break the rules is not natural. And so he gives a rather complicated story about why we experience this sentiment: one that involves sympathy, an instinct for self-defense, and a recognition that it’s in our individual interest to do what servers our collective interests-all of which, by contrast, he takes to be natural” (p 132)

Moral theories have always had intuitionist elements. Those who claim against it prove it best.

### Waterless Moat (a discussion of some philosophers)

In chapter 1 Appiah brings the importance of moral language directly at the philosophical community itself. For him philosophers (some) used to do everything everyone else did in the sciences. They used to be part of the public, social, inspectable moral language but now philosophy has shrunk to a private language. These are Appiah’s claims in about Ivory Twer philosophy and they are serious especially in the context the moral language argument: if philosophy is not publically accessible, socially active and open to inspection by other fields then the conversation of philosophy fails to be a moral language.

This return to moral language will not be painless 

> “What happens when moral theory is called before the tribunal of psychology-when facts interrogates value? Will the blurring of boundaries advance the aims of ethics or lead to its eclipse?...Can moral philosophy be naturalized?” (p 32)

Appiah advocates how philosophy could become ‘naturalized’ by his very own writing: he communicates with examples from many fields ranging from literature to experimental psychology. If you take one of the responsiblities of the ethical philosopher to be to investigate the ethical nature of reality then why shouldn’t the ethical philosopher draw from all parts of that reality and in fact be immersed in it? 

The contrary state could be described by continuing the waterless moat analogy: Philosophy is a castle with big walls and a waterless moat: Philosophy is built to protect it from attack. But really nobody is attacking from outside. The rest of the sciences are too busy using the water to farm. From the high walls impenetrable by sincerity (only laadders of vocabulary can get you up) members of the castle look down upon the farmers, visible for miles because of high vantage point, and describe to each other how the farmers farm differently. Best of all they can put the farmers in moral categories and maybe rank them. Whenever the farmer shares a neighborly interest the philosophers throw buckets of words at them! Contrast this with whenever an army really does come to attack: Philosophers band together and throw so many essays the ground becomes slippery. It’s less confusing to just keep sending the hermits their tribut. (It has got to hurt my grade to have so much fun in a paper) Unfortunately (for this paper) my personal experience with philosophy doesn’t include an example that fits this story. I’ve accepted Appiah’s description based on the merit of reputation, his clear mastery of so many fields in philosophy, and my experience that humans can be this way because of the isolated moral language in Economics.

The point is that Appiah thinks philosophy should be in the actual world and language is how to do it and prove it. Arguments must incorporate what others say and do. When a psychology study concludes that morality is situational the philosopher should of course critique the structure of the experiments (always heavily critiqueable) but also consider its implications. The Virtue Ethicist should not just reject the situationalist challenge by any conceptual means but actually consider that any claim of statically virtuous character must be amended. The study Appiah cities criticizes Virtue Ethics is in addition to the much greater work of forcing a response.

### Data and moral language

Experiments with data are different then thought experiments. Data driven experiments invoke a historical inheritance of the widely respected tradition of science as a public language. This includes a decentralization of authority and a impeachabliness of ‘data’. Most importantly data sets allow for a strongly inspectable language even across cultural barriers.

For Science anyone can be wrong and anyone can be right. If this is true scientists should listen because they are supposed to be egoless when it comes to their conversations. Why is this important? If scientist has an oversized ego then they don’t listen. If they don’t listen then their language is not open for inspection and so it would not be good moral language. By invocating data Appiah supports an open inspection of language. If egos are the most difficult obstacle in the way of communication this influence might be the most important. 

Excellent moral peer communication requires egolessness because peer-review, another embedded feature of Appiah’s reference to data and science, is about more than judgments shared. The communication of peer review, the building together of concepts, closely aligns with the moral language he presents. Appiah doesn’t dismiss concepts outright because of flaws or disagreements but works with the text. When discussing evaluative language he says “...virtue ethicists often sound as if they’re courting tautology. It’s bad to be vile and beastly; good to be noble...” (p 157) but then says later “We use values not only to think for ourselves but also to guide one another.” (p 157) It is not an easy to quiet the ego and listen for value in a mainly disagreeable argument but Appiah is committed to this process.

Why is data admissible to ethical discussion? Experiments of people’s actual moral responses to actual circumstances with other people in the world are important for Appiah’s project because it serves his ethical mission of moral language smoothly. He says “Here is the data that has been interpreted this way. Lets talk about the interpretation.” instead of “Before I bring in the data Is it ever ok to consider data when discussing ethics?”  The former is so smooth I do not remember a single questioning of data itself as being ethically admissible during the entire reading of the book. 

And finally the reference to data invokes another important aspect of moral language for Appiah: a direct claim of cross cultural relevance. Appiah is not interested in describing moral systems for sub-cultures or small communities in isolation. The question is how we correctly construct our ethical framework within all contexts. Consider the entrenched a sub-culture moral conflict with the greater culture at large that occurs on page 141 where Jim the “harmless” botanist is in another country and finds himself in moral confusion concerning the life and death. Nowhere in Appiah’s discussion of the situation is the concept mentioned that maybe Jim should just go with the flow and do as the Romans do. Just as the possibility of absolute cultural relativism is ignored (rightly so) in Jim’s case when data is referenced the same exercise is being done. This is data about humanity, he implies, and it applies to everyone. The reader and all others must respond to it.

The title of the book is ‘Experiments in Ethics’ and the implications of using data of the experiments in philosophical discussion shouldn’t be discounted as just neat. It is strongly supporting of the framing of moral language for Appiah. 

### Conclusion

From a beginning investigation in muddled thought I was able to build up an understanding of Appiah’s use of moral language and its integral need for his ethical project. His challenge of Virtue ethics would have been impossible without the social being unremovable from language. The controlled demolition of Intuitionism required the need for justification of any inspection. “What do you know about your intuition again?” The challenge to philosophy to change its ways was a challenge to speak a moral language and get out of the castle. The use of data carried with it the characteristics of moral language he wanted while enlisting the respect of the sciences. A very smart move.
