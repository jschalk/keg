Emmanuel began class by asking for clarification between the use of the words truth and reality, being that truth is a matter of perception and reality something that, especially in a phenomenological lens, an individual may not know for sure is the case. Emmanuel further contested the use of the word rationl in exchange for the word ethical in that rationl may imply hierarchy for Levinas. After this was resolved, Dr. Simon led the class down another path and claimed that exploitation of a being is when objectification occurs, not one moment before it. Fundamentally, humans must exploit, and the ethical action can then be self-realization of exploitation and the ceasing of it. This process of keeping one’s power to exploit in check is a sufficient condition for ethicality in Levinas, but not necessary. Dr. Simon additionally claimed that Levinas says that Ethics must come before politics, similar to, but not mimicking Aristotle when Aristotle presupposes “man is a political animal.”

Dr. Simon refocused the discussion to include historical texts to clarify that most cultures have a messianic figure, but not all as was suggested in the protocol from the week prior. On a related note, he continued to discuss a study that religion often tries to explain, eschatology. Dr. Simon suggested that eschatology cannot be based upon scientific principles since the inductive method used in science necessarily entails the observer to be describing the past and present, but never the future. Eschatology, the study of end-times, therefore, must not be able to use science in its predictions. Dr. Simon moved the discussion forward to say that eschatology must proceed without science, taking strongly into account how humans will be able to effect the world around us, obviously affecting the future of that world. He then took this opportunity to take aim at climate change non-believers. Dr. Simon claimed that ontology is restricted to the past and present, but eschatology opens up analysis for the future. Totalizers tend to think that “we don’t know, yet,” whereas non-totalizers tend to think that “we may never know,” arriving back indirectly to Emmanuel's original distinction between truth and reality. There are some things we may never know, to include other’s secret thoughts, which Dr. Simon threatened to torture the student’s for following his claim.

Still finding ways to side step the text, the point was brought up that Levinas spoke of discourse between two humans as religious. Dr. Simon, shedding light on the odd use of this term, read “religious” as an indicator that people are being bound together by discourse, just as people bind together around religion. Dr. Simon then almost got back to the text, but then began to speak rather intently upon the distinction between “absolutely knowing,” and “knowing the absolute.” Absolutely knowing refers to a person’s conviction in the truth of a matter, though it may not actually be true, and the latter refers to having knowledge of something that will forever be true.

Discussing the text, the first class expert duly framed the Levinasian concepts that, no matter what, humans are separate from each other, and that we can never know what infinity is, but it is something we still engage with (somehow). Dr. Simon says that Levinas uses some elements of Descartes to relate to us these hard to define concepts, although he rejects the “substance talk.” Levinas does, however, use the “experience” talk, referring to “seeing.” He also keeps the Cartesian textual structure that moves the reader to think. 

A discussion ensued concerning the main claim of the section, “Separation and Discourse,” correlation does not suffice as a category for transcendence. On the same note it was stated/quoted that, for separation between the Other and I, a positive, real-world movement is necessary.  Shortly thereafter, a discussion concerning topics that I don’t understand occurred. The claim was that, according to Dr. Simon, Levinas wanted to critique the Hegellian dialectic. Dr. Simon then stated that having an analytic value in terms of A=A, a tautology, is not a problem, but the truth-value does not refer to tautological statements.

The class expert then highlighted two important lines, the first of which defined the asymmetrical moral claim, “what I permit myself to demand of myself is not comparable with what I have the right to demand of the Other,” and the second was indicated by the first, “[There is] the radical impossibility of seeing oneself from the outside and of speaking in the same sense of oneself…” These are two fundamentals of Levinasian ethics, both of which rely on the concept of interiority. The term psychism was defined as “inner-life” or “interiority.” These pyschisms comprise individuality.

A general discussion of the claim, phenomenological experience of a time period is necessary to understanding a time period, occurred next. This is based off the fact that when a historian, “the survivor” as Levinas would say, looks back at history and the events that occurred, they must largely judge without having access to the subjective experience of those that underwent the events. In other words, history as we know it is simply what is permitted and may or may not accurately reflect what actually occurred.  A little later on, an effective bumper sticker for this inability to access the subjective experience was produced by Dr. Simon: “The ‘I’ in Interiority exists unrecognized.” This not only has implications for history, but a range of political situations, including the justice system.

A similar conversation occurred right after this; it was similar to, but not immediately related to the prior, and consisted of the notion that an individual must break with “participation” in the world around, calling into question those concepts she has conviction in in order to strengthen the remaining convictions. Levinas calls this break with participation a state of atheism. He says that in this state of atheism, one is separated from metaphysics itself, liberating the individual entirely from outside influence, subjecting her to only her spontaneity. To be an adult, to appreciate one’s freedom, one must break with all outside participation. One must go through separation.

A discussion on Desire occurred in which the bottom lines were that the Other makes an individual happy, but the desire for the Other is insatiable. Desire is the way to come to appreciate the inviolability of the Other. Levinas includes the great line, “the distance that separates happiness from desire separates politics from religion.” Through politics, both negotiating party’s desires are satisfied. Religion, taken in the earlier established context, is desire and ensures asymmetrical enjoyment: “Religion is… not struggle for recognition.” This asymmetrical enjoyment binds communities together.

At this point, the conversation began to follow a format where we highlighted the different sections of the book, and thus the notes will reflect this straight to the point approach. The section B.3, “Discourse,” discerned speaking as the revealing of the Other. This is where “meaning is found,” to paraphrase Dr. Simon. Discourse opens up the realm where the Other is called to speak. When I am listening, I am questioning my own perception because I respect the Other and am allowing them to potentially change my perception. This relationship is non-Socratic in that it relies on actual content being taught, versus the Socratic method, which typically left the victim of maieutic questioning with knowledge they already held, sans dignity.  The next section, “Rhetoric and Injustice” was reduced down to the concept that rhetoric is merely a tool for violation and manipulation, resisting this true, meaningful discourse.

B.5, “Discourse and Ethics,” presumed that when the Other speaks, she breaks up patterns of oration and the politics of coherence innate in the individual engaged in discourse with her. The Other, qua Other, does not fit into another individual’s system. Dominant patterns of coherence internal to the individual dictate marginalization of the Other. Therefore, when one engages in true discourse with the Other, one does not dismiss the Other by fitting her into the categories created by a pattern of coherence, a category of thought. This is what engaging the Other who is “nude” means. Nudity here is the concept that an individual engages the Other without putting any labels on them, metaphorically covering her up. When one engages with the Other, one must put aside all self-regard. An important result of this defenseless, asymmetrical thinking, then, is that the abolition of a number of untouchable rights, including alleged untouchable property rights, occurs with the Desire of the Other. This section is mainly about being concerned with giving and how the individual is called to give with no self-regard when we engage with the Other. This calling stems from our Desire of the Other qua Other. When one does give in this way, one receives enjoyment of the Other, thus instilling meaning and purpose into the giving party’s life. This reward is an effect of giving, and is not meant to function as a reason to give. It is merely a byproduct of the asymmetrical relationship to the Other, not a cause to pursue it.

Dr. Simon claimed that, at this point in the book, the text solidifies a thesis, which it had been building up to until then. This book is an Ethical Metaphysics. This ethics is concerned with inter-human relationships. It is based upon the claim that humans are all forever separate, forever Alterior. The book looks ahead, at the end of section B.7, to its eventual development of the thesis, “The conjuncture of the same and the other, in which even their verbal proximity is maintained, is the direct and full face welcome of the other by me.” 

In the following part of the book, C, “Truth and Justice,” Levinas begins to defend his claim by attacking a surprising number of beliefs of what an American audience would consider to be its social norms. This section in particular reduces freedom into spontaneity. The temporal connotations of the word “spontaneity” are important here because it reduces actions taken by a free individual to being made on the spot or in the moment, and therefore not subject to previously held convictions. If an individual acts on a rationl thought process or systematic thinking in this way, her freedom is not really freedom but a constricted result produced by the refining mechanizations of thought. To act spontaneously, then, is to be entirely free, to act without regard to anything or anyone, even one’s own prior held notions. Based on this move, Levinas then claims that it is when the agent checks her own spontaneity out of respect for the Other that ethics takes place. Self-critique of freedom is the measured respect of the Other. Moral consciousness then is not theoretical, but instead it can only occur in the realization of the harms of freedom. Morality begins not by justification for one’s actions, but when freedom “feels itself to be arbitrary and violent.” It questions the basis for acting instead of merely questioning the action.

One major epistemological source for an individual in Levinas system is the Other. That is, for an individual to learn anything objective to itself, it is necessarily reliant on an Other to shed light on it. This is not to say that either party arrives at reality, but they do both benefit by having objective opinions on said reality that they would not otherwise have access to. The only way to gain access to this objective opinion is to completely submit to the Other’s point of view. We concluded the session by discussing the avoidance of the Hegellian doughnut diet, and with thirteen pages left of the prior week’s reading to filter through.
