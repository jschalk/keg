# Kurt, Walter, and Horst

In the World War One, of my distant relatives, Abdarrahman, was captured by the Germans and spent some time in captivity. He was sent to the countryside to work on a farm. There, he started seeing the daughter of farm owners, Emilie, and eventually married her.

I believe that her parents were not perfectly happy about their relationship. This may be one reason why having spent some years in Germany, and having their first kid, Kurt, there, they would eventually move to the USSR. First, they settled in Tatarstan, where they had their second kid, Walter. And, in the end, they would relocate to Bashkortostan where they had the third one, Horst.

Perhaps, moving back from Germany to the USSR was not the best idea, retrospectively speaking. Proudly displaying the photo of Emilie’s brother, the officer of Wehrmacht in their kitchen, may have been an even worse one. In the late 1930s, they were both arrested, and Abdarrahman would be beaten to death during the interrogation. Emilie had to spend many years in the Gulag, not a particularly pleasant experience, especially in the grim and hard years of the World War II.

Their sons all served in the Soviet army, and all fought against Germany, in the World War. But - their fates varied. Once Soviets reached German territory, Kurt was ordered to stop there. He was not allowed to even set his foot on the German soil. Walter and Horst, however, were allowed in, and marched with the Soviet army deep into Germany.

Why? Well, that is pretty obvious. It were their ID papers that defined their status, and defined how the Soviet state was seeing them. Now the thing is, Soviet ID papers had an (extremely important!) ethnicity section. Your ethnicity was written straight in your passport, and which ethnicity is written there, was all-consequential for your future.

Technically, three brothers were born from the same (Tatar) father, and same (German) mother. But they were born in three different places:

Kurt was born in Germany, and for the Soviet state he was a German

Walter was born in Tatarstan, so he would be a Tatar

And Horst, born in Bashkortostan, would be designated as a Bashkir

So, naturally, Soviet commanders (or rather, security officers) were extremely alarmed about the prospects of an ethnically German (based on his papers) Kurt entering into Germany, where he might potentially defect or collaborate with the locals. But his Tatar and Bashkir (all based on their papers), brothers presented no security threat, and were allowed in, without further questions.

I think this story serves as a not bad illustration on how the state is seeing stuff.

Bureaucracy can not deal with the world in its whole complexity.

To apply the standard protocol it must first assign an object into one of its standard categories.

How is it assigned into a category, and based on what, does not concern the state very much. Its institutions can absolutely do it in a semi-random, arbitrary manner. What is important here, is that assignment into a standard category had been executed in some way.

Now when this job is done, an object becomes just a figure, on paper/screen. Now it can be dealt with as a part of quantitative data, and quantitative reasoning (unlike the qualitative one) is something the state can do, and understand.
