As a psychologist, I’ve spent a lot of time with people who hold beliefs I find hard to sit with. And one of the first things you learn doing this job is that change is near impossible when the work is framed by a judgment-laden agenda that categorizes the person rather than trying to understand them. This orientation is not about avoiding accountability for harmful behavior or toxic beliefs (that’s important, too), but rather, to discover why those behaviors and beliefs developed in the first place—because that’s where growth and healing can happen.

Like a lot of liberals, I’m frequently perplexed why anyone would vote for a Republican. It seems painfully obvious to me that our shared problems are exacerbated—if not directly caused by—laws that Republicans enact. The data is certainly on our side here. We can see a direct line between conservative policies and flattened wages, spiked costs in healthcare and education, outrageous social injustices, multiple economic emergencies, and disastrous changes to our climate. Why would anyone keep voting for all this?

It turns out, we have answers to this question.

I imagine the first word that popped into your head was “racism”. And you’d be right. Studies do consistently show that racial resentment is one of the strongest single predictors of Republican vote choice in the modern era. But what’s driving this racial resentment?

---

### Social Dominance Orientation

**Social dominance orientation**, or SDO, describes the degree to which a person prefers a world sorted into groups on top and groups underneath. Before you say, “that’s just a fancy way of saying racism”—no, it isn’t. Because the sorting criterion isn’t always race; it can be religious affiliation, caste, nationality, gender, or, honestly, any measure by which people can be separated into groups.

The measure of SDO comes out of work by Felicia Pratto, Jim Sidanius and their colleagues in the 1990s (Pratto et al., 1994; Sidanius & Pratto, 1999). What it’s actually measuring is a belief about **structure**. Not a belief about Black people or immigrants or the poor (which is why so many will deny being bigots and genuinely believe it), but a belief that some arrangement of above-and-below is natural and necessary, and that flattening it produces disorder rather than justice. High-SDO people aren’t necessarily hostile to anyone in particular. They’re uneasy about social engineering efforts to promote equality in principle.

So it’s no surprise that the measure predicts opposition to downward redistribution, to a broad social safety net, to immigration, to civil rights enforcement—and it predicts a range of hierarchy-enhancing political attitudes even after accounting for other individual differences. Pratto and colleagues (1994) also found occupational sorting: people higher in SDO tend to prefer hierarchy-enhancing institutions and roles, while those lower in SDO are more attracted to hierarchy-attenuating ones.

There are millions of Americans who would materially benefit from policies they vote against based on a genuine conviction that a world where the ladder gets flattened is a worse world, including for them. This includes people at the bottom of the hierarchy, too. There are plenty of people getting the worst of an arrangement who still believe the arrangement is basically correct—or at least that it’s better to stay at the bottom if that means preventing certain others from rising up.

SDO is a value premise, and everyone sits somewhere on that spectrum. Given a starting belief that hierarchy is what holds a society together, much of what follows becomes more comprehensible. Political disputes over immigration, race, gender, religion and sexuality can all be understood, at least partly, as conflicts over existing social hierarchies and whether they should be preserved or dismantled.

But hierarchy doesn’t tell the whole story. Because before you can worry about which groups should be above which other groups, you have to decide something even more fundamental:

**Who are my people?**

### Politics as Group Identity

Human beings are profoundly social animals, and political parties aren’t simply collections of policy positions. They are also **groups**.

Henri Tajfel and his colleagues (1971) famously demonstrated just how little it takes to produce an us and a them. In their “minimal group” experiments, even essentially arbitrary group assignments were enough to produce measurable favoritism toward one’s own group.

Once a group identity becomes psychologically important, we tend to favor our own group, interpret ambiguous information in its favor, and judge outsiders less charitably. *(Note: I actually see this phenomenon here all the time, so those on the left are hardly exempt)*.

American partisanship has become especially potent because political identity increasingly overlaps with other social identities. Political scientist Lilliana Mason (2018) calls this *social sorting*. Race, religion, ideology, geography and culture increasingly line up with partisan identity rather than cutting across it. Her research describes a process in which separate identities accumulate into something approaching a partisan mega-identity.

Someone isn’t necessarily just a Republican. Republican identity may be intertwined with being evangelical Christian, rural, a gun owner, culturally traditional, distrustful of universities and professional expertise, and embedded in a family or community where most people share those identities.

Politics becomes woven into a much larger answer to the question: *“What kind of person am I?”*

From the outside, we may think we’re asking someone to support some policy that might save them $3,000 a year. From the inside, the choice may feel more like: *betray your family, your church, your neighbors, your values and people like you*—and join those people who look down on all of you. The extra $3,000 barely stands a chance.

This also helps explain why arguments about “voting against your own interests” so often go nowhere.

Because belonging is an interest. Identity is an interest. Perceived status is an interest. Protecting the community you identify with is an interest. Maintaining a world that makes moral and cultural sense to you is an interest.

Once politics becomes identity, voting is no longer simply about what government will do for you.

It is also about *who you are.*

### System Justification

But why would someone near the bottom of a hierarchy defend the hierarchy itself?

Psychologists John Jost and Mahzarin Banaji (1994) proposed *system justification theory* to help explain exactly that phenomenon: the tendency, under some circumstances, to defend and legitimize existing social, economic and political arrangements—even when those arrangements disadvantage us.

Believing that the system around you is fundamentally unjust carries some uncomfortable implications. Maybe hard work doesn’t reliably produce success. Maybe authority isn’t necessarily legitimate. Maybe deserving people lose. Maybe the rules aren’t fair. Maybe institutions you’ve trusted your entire life aren’t worthy of that trust.

Those conclusions carry psychological costs. System justification theory proposes that people are motivated not only to defend themselves and their groups, but sometimes to defend the legitimacy of the larger system in which they live. Research has found system-justifying processes even among disadvantaged groups, although the strength of those effects depends substantially on context.

Thus, a struggling blue-collar worker can simultaneously believe: *I’m getting screwed*, and: *The America I believe in rewards merit and hard work.*

Those ideas create tension, but the tension doesn’t have to be resolved by rejecting the economic system. It can instead be resolved by locating the problem somewhere else: immigrants, DEI, welfare recipients, government regulation, urban elites, Hollywood, socialism, or people who supposedly aren’t playing by the rules.

The system remains legitimate. Someone else becomes responsible for its failures.

### Threat Response

Conservatives and liberals also differ, on average, in how they respond to certain kinds of threat and disorder.

John Hibbing, Kevin Smith and John Alford (2014) reviewed evidence linking political conservatism with greater responsiveness to threatening, aversive, unfamiliar and disorderly stimuli. Some of the stronger claims about a generalized biological “negativity bias” have been debated, so I wouldn’t reduce this to conservatives are *simply more fearful*. The more defensible point is that threat, instability and disruption tend to carry greater political weight for people who place a high value on order and continuity.

And conservatism is, almost by definition, concerned with preservation: maintaining institutions, traditions, boundaries and established social arrangements.

Things that destabilize those arrangements therefore carry particular political significance—rapid demographic change, immigration, changing racial hierarchies, changing sexual and gender norms, and religious decline.

Messages framed around *threat to the existing social order* can consequently resonate especially strongly with people predisposed to value stability, conformity and continuity.

Notice how neatly this intersects with SDO and group identity. First establish that there is an *us*. Then establish a hierarchy in which our group occupies its proper place. Now introduce the perception that the hierarchy is *changing*.

Suddenly politics isn’t about marginal tax rates anymore. It’s about **loss**: *We are losing our country. We are losing our culture. We are losing our neighborhoods. We are losing control.*

And once politics becomes organized around perceived threat, another psychological disposition becomes particularly important.

---

Authoritarianism

Political psychologists Marc Hetherington and Jonathan Weiler (2009) studied what they call an *authoritarian predisposition*. The name can be misleading. It doesn’t simply mean “likes dictators.” At its core, authoritarianism concerns the relative importance people place on social conformity, cohesion and order versus individual autonomy and difference.

People higher in authoritarian predisposition tend to place greater value on obedience, established norms and social uniformity. People lower in authoritarianism tend to place greater value on independence, heterogeneity and individual autonomy.

Under ordinary conditions, those differences may not dominate politics. **Threat changes that.**

Hetherington and Weiler (2009) found that perceived threat activates authoritarian predispositions politically. Order becomes more important. Strong leadership becomes more attractive. Deviance becomes more threatening. Outgroups become more suspect. And exercises of government power that might otherwise seem intrusive or excessive can become acceptable—sometimes desirable—if they promise to restore control.

This helps explain something liberals often experience as bewildering hypocrisy. People who spent decades insisting on “limited government” can embrace aggressive uses of state power when that power is directed toward people, institutions or social changes they perceive as threatening.

Years of outrage over “cancel culture” can coexist with support for an administration that has repeatedly used state power to suppress political speech.

Likewise, decades of conservative warnings about government interference in private enterprise did not prevent the Trump administration from arranging for the federal government to acquire roughly a 10% equity stake in Intel in 2025.

And if the deepest commitment were really government *should be small or federal spending should be restrained*, the Republican Congress would presumably have recoiled from spending tens of billions of dollars to dramatically expand ICE into a fascistic secret police.

These positions look wildly inconsistent if the underlying principles are really *small government, fiscal restraint, free speech, and keeping government out of people’s lives.*

They become considerably more coherent if the deeper principle is something else: **Government should defend the social order I value.**

From that perspective, the apparent contradiction largely disappears. Government power is objectionable when it restrains “*us*”, disrupts established hierarchies, or imposes unwanted social change. But government power can become entirely acceptable when it punishes “*them*“, reinforces traditional boundaries, or promises to restore a threatened order.

And underneath that concern is often a zero-sum assumption: power gained by *them* is power lost by *us*.

### Motivated Reasoning

Authoritarianism helps explain why supposedly bedrock principles can become flexible when social order feels threatened. But that leaves another question:

**How does someone make that switch without experiencing themselves as a hypocrite?**

This is where *motivated reasoning comes in.*

We all like to imagine reasoning as gathering evidence, weighing it fairly, and then reaching a conclusion. Often, though, we begin with a conclusion we want to be true—especially when identity is involved—and reason backward from there.

Charles Taber and Milton Lodge (2006) found that people judged arguments supporting their existing political beliefs as stronger, scrutinized opposing arguments more aggressively, and preferentially sought congenial information. In some cases, exposure to competing evidence left people more polarized rather than less.

And this isn’t uniquely conservative. Liberals do it, too. A meta-analysis by Peter Ditto and colleagues (2019) found roughly equivalent partisan bias on the left and right. The psychological vulnerability is bipartisan, my brothers and sisters. We need to remember that.

**But the political environment in which that vulnerability operates isn’t symmetrical.**

Yochai Benkler, Robert Faris and Hal Roberts (2018), analyzing millions of political stories and social-media posts, described a particularly insular right-wing media ecosystem in which partisan sources repeatedly reinforced one another while teaching audiences to distrust outside institutions that might correct false claims.

More recent work by Mohsen Mosleh and colleagues (2024) found the same behavioral asymmetry across multiple datasets: conservative and Republican-leaning social-media users shared substantially more low-quality information than liberal users—even when the quality of the information was judged not by professional fact-checkers but by politically balanced groups of ordinary citizens, or even by Republican participants themselves.

So the problem isn’t that Republican brains possess some unique defect. It’s that a universal human weakness is operating inside an extremely effective reinforcement system. Motivated reasoning works particularly well when your preferred conclusion is repeated by your political leaders, affirmed by the media you trust, echoed by your social group, and contrary information can be dismissed as coming from corrupt or hostile institutions.

That is how the principle stops determining the conclusion and the desired conclusion starts determining how the principle gets applied. When “*they*” expand federal power, it’s tyranny; when “*we*” do it, it’s patriotism. When “*they*” restrict speech, it’s censorship; when “*we*” do it, it’s pwning the libs. “*Their*” spending proves irresponsibility; “*ours*” was a common sense investment.

That’s why exposing hypocrisy so rarely changes minds. We see a contradiction and assume the person must either abandon the behavior or abandon the principle. Motivated reasoning offers a third option: *Explain why this time is different.*

### Putting it together

GOP policies undermine more than people’s economic interests. We’re also talking about interests that touch nearly every basic element of daily life: how long people live, whether they can access medical care, how safe they and their families are, the opportunities their children receive, and the quality of the air, water, and communities in which they live. On virtually every metric that can be measured, Democrats offer better solutions. So, why do people keep voting for Republicans?

The psychology we’ve reviewed helps explain why. **Social dominance orientation** overemphasizes the value of hierarchy and is often the cognitive engine that fuels racism, misogyny, xenophobia, and prejudice toward LGBTQ people. **Group identity** turns politics into us versus them—and identities embedded in family, religion, community, and culture are extraordinarily difficult to break. **System justification** legitimizes the existing order even when it produces bad outcomes. **Threat** makes social change feel like loss. **Authoritarianism** makes restoring order more important than limiting government power. And **motivated reasoning** allows the resulting contradictions to be explained away.

Put together, these mechanisms suggest that people aren’t voting without regard to their interests so much as prioritizing different interests. **Identity, belonging, perceived status and hierarchy, and preservation of a preferred social order** can outweigh money, healthcare, education, public safety, environmental quality, or even longevity. And these priorities are continually reinforced by a giant, insular right-wing media ecosystem that supplies narratives of threat, grievance, and outgroup blame while providing ready-made explanations for dismissing contradictory evidence when it can’t simply be ignored.

And before you reply with, ‘*Nah, it’s just racism*,’ remember that racism cannot by itself explain the Republican coalition. In 2024, Trump won about 15% of Black voters (30% of Black men under 45), 48% of Hispanic voters, and 40% of Asian voters according to Pew’s analysis of validated voters (Hartig et al., 2025, Associated Press, 2024). Those numbers certainly don’t mean race was irrelevant—racial resentment remains an important predictor of Republican support—but they do mean that racism alone cannot explain why tens of millions of Americans, including substantial numbers of racial minorities, vote Republican. The broader psychological machinery matters too: hierarchy, group identity, perceived threat, authoritarianism, and motivated reasoning.

<div align="center">

### Indeed, over the last fifty years, the Republican Party has been deliberately engineered less around a concrete set of foundational principles than around a coalition and messaging system designed to exploit precisely these psychological vulnerabilities: higher social dominance orientation and attachment to existing hierarchies; multiple overlapping in-group identities that Republican strategists purposefully stitched together; heightened responsiveness to threat, disorder, and social change; and greater receptivity to authoritarian solutions when the established social order feels endangered. Race, evangelical Christianity, gender, guns, nationalism, sexuality, and cultural grievance were not simply constituencies that happened to converge under the Republican umbrella. Party strategists repeatedly worked to bind them together, reinforce a common sense of us, identify threatening thems, and keep those threats politically active.

</div>

This is why the Republican Party, as it now exists, needs to be burned to the ground, along with its right-wing media ecosystem. This cannot happen legislatively—it has to happen culturally. And that will only happen if Democrats and progressives more generally begin to employ tactics that can override these psychological influences, at least among enough voters that MAGA will no longer be able to win power.
