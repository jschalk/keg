Here I want to suggest you an exercise. You can (and I think you should) organise yourself a month-long course in political theory. How? By reading Ronald Syme The Roman Revolution.

It is a very, very intense and unsentimental book covering the political transition of Rome, from Republic to Empire, and centred around the personal career of Octavian Augustus, a small-town bourgeois from the province to become the First Emperor.

It is very rarely that I see a book so informative, and more importantly so realistic when it comes to the representation of *political mechanics*. Of how the things actually work in this world, based on the empirical evidence we have.

You see, when people say ‘political theory’, what they usually mean is some kind of intellectual history (“what intellectuals have been writing on this or that”). Cool enough, but that is not what we are talking about.

And when they say ‘political science’, they are usually referring to a modern pseudo-science, a poor & thoughtless imitation of natural sciences, driven primarily by the penis envy of humanities departments towards the STEM folk.

Neither of these systems of thought gives a good understanding of how the politics work.

You must expect that any realistic account, will be almost by definition historical, because it will be based on the empirical evidence, and all the empirical evidence we have, belongs to the past. And yet, for the most part, historians fail to deliver, because they (most of them) are simply not brilliant enough.

You see, there is a very common problem among the historians. They are smart, conscientious, hardworking. Their material can be interesting, and genuinely curious to read. But whenever they step aside from quoting/describing their sources and try to make some kind of personal judgement, it strikes you as a mix of stupid and naive. And, since analysis is inseparable from the art of making a judgement, in the end you find their conclusions disappointingly dull.

They can dig out some interesting factoids. What they can’t do is generalise.

Now the book of Syme, is an exceedingly rare example of a structured and methodical analysis of the empirical evidence, showing the *general patterns* of political life, be it in the Principate-era Rome, or in the North America of nowadays. Much of what Syme is writing about, is not place- and era-specific, but more or less universal, and can be applied to the great many cases across times and ages.

Reading of Caesar, reading of August, will help you to better understand Mao Zedong. In fact, reading of Caesar and August may be the shortcut for understanding Mao Zedong. That is partially because the age of Caesars is distant enough, that we can treat it impartially, in an analytical and cold-blooded way. With Mao, or Lenin, on the other hand, we just… can’t. They are too close to us, and our lenses get fogged by the ideological holy wars of nowadays.

I would even say that the history of ancient Rome may be occupying a sweet spot: distant enough to be viewed neutrally, and without too much of a partisan bias, and close enough to be understandable and relatable to a modern-day person. That makes it a great starting point, to be learning the politics from. Start with Sulla, start with Marius, and from there move towards Lenin and Kerensky. Not the other way around.

But you don’t have to study Rome, merely for its own sake. What matters, and what interests us the most is general patterns, of human history and human politics.

You can see the brilliance of Syme, on how he makes his judgements, and, especially, his general remarks. I will give you some of the shorter, and more aphoristic ones:

*“Whatever the form and name of government, an oligarchy lurks behind the façade”*

*“Without a party a statesman is nothing”*

*“A democracy cannot rule an empire”*

*“ No oligarchy could survive if its members refused to abide by the rules”*

*“Naked despotism is vulnerable.”*

Each of them deserves (and will get) a detailed commentary, but later. For now, I will only add that it is a pretty harsh and unsentimental view on politics, but - at the same time, a view very far from the primitive machiavellianism, “might-makes-right” style. That is partially because might-makes-right paradigm raises more questions than it answers. Like, okay, might makes right, that is fine. But what makes might?

Now Syme sets to answer that very difficult, and paradoxical question. And his book has great many clues, on what makes might, and what the might even is, politically speaking. That I will be covering later. For now I will only state that Syme is a master of both making a judgement, and presenting it to the audience. Notice, how his judgements can be very explicit, very concise and also - often very aphotistic.

They count for only a very small part of his book, and yet they make a centrally important element of it.

> Here is an idea for an exercise. Use a special colour to underline/highlight all the personal judgements of Syme, his generalisations. And in the end, you will have a brief of a political theory of his own. Which will look a lil bit like a red letter bible, except it’s not a bible.

Now there is another thing I like about this author. Unlike in the conventions of modern trade books, Syme gives tons of quotes & citations **many of which are not even translated to English.** Some of them in Latin, some of them in Greek. So, in a footnote you will often see a long quote written with strange Greek letters, and you won’t even understand what it means.

And that is great!

That is great, because it shows you both the taste & flavour of the ancient Roman culture and the **scale of your own ignorance.** You will see how dumb you are. Before reading Syme, I, for example, never imagined that much of the ancient Latin literature and private correspondence sounds like the dialogues from the *War and Peace*.

If you have read Leo Tolstoy in original, you will know that a huge part of his book is written in French. Almost 3% of the total volume. That is because Tolstoy has been writing the dialogues of Russian aristocrats, taking place in the salons of St Petersburg and Moscow, as they actually happened historically, in French.

Tolstoy aimed to represent how the actual, historical princes and counts of the Russian Empire were conversing with each other. As a result, you have entire pages of the aristocratic dialogues written in French, with only few Russian words here and there. That is historically accurate, and represents the reality of the Russian Empire, run by the largely foreign, or at least culturally foreign ruling class[^1].

All these princes and counts have been raised on the French culture, educated on it. They can speak Russian, of course, but whenever they need to say something smart, abstract, or, in any way sophisticated, they have to say it in French. French is the original, and the Russian high culture is a reverberation of it.

So when you see the long untranslated quotes from the ancient Roman authors and politicians, you cannot help but draw the parallels with Tolstoy. It is Latin, of course, but Latin with huge number of Greeks insertions, and quotations. You cannot but contemplate how the elite education in late republican Rome was based on the Greek, and how the entire high culture was permeated by the Greek ideas, Greek stories, Greek narratives.

Once you realise that the entire Greek culture was just one endless terrorist propaganda

In Athens, they erected a monument to the τυραννόκτονοι, Harmodius and Aristogeiton, who murdered a a tyrant Hipparchus. They put it straight on the main square, on Agora, so central was this narrative for their identity. Nothing sweeter, nothing nobler than the death of a tyrant, and, usurping the power over the city does make one a tyrant, no matter how nice, or how horrible he may be as a person.

Usurping the power does make you an enemy combatant, a subject for an assassination. And the free-spirited, patriotic citizens have a moral duty, an imperative to kill you.

Now once you realise that Brutus, and other Roman nobles, were brought up on the Greek ideals, were educated in Athens, were quoting the Greek authors every 5 minutes and could not express any complicated thought or idea without inserting the Greek terms here, and there, the murder of Caesar becomes very much more understandable. Reading the Greeks, emulating the Greeks, Brutus and Cassius must have imagined themselves as a sort of Harmodius and Aristogeiton. They, too, wanted to dip their knife in the blood of an usurper, like their heroes did.

The entire Republican narrative and republican mythology of Rome must have had a lot to do with the fact that the posh youth were raised on the Greek culture, and were consuming it in the actual, untranslated original, much like the Russian nobles did with the French.

Take for example, the poetic lines of Alexander Pushkin that every educated Russian knows by memory, because they comprise a part of the compulsory school canon,

> “We shall amuse good citizens
And on the pilar of shame
We shall strangle the last king
With the intestine of the last cleric”
>
>(Alexander Pushkin, 1819)

These lines are quite representative of how much of the highly privileged youth in St Petersburg felt, in the wake of Napoleonic Wars. Kill, kill, kill, kill the King. Kill the royal family. Kill them all.

<div align="center"><img src="images/russian_calvary_painting.webp" alt="Painting of Russian army in St. Petersburg 1825" width="600"></div>


*That provides lots of historical context, on the intellectual & ideological atmosphere just before the Decembrist revolt of 1825. By that point, the murder of King, and the extermination of the royals, became a fashionable conversation topic between the posh youth, many of whom served as officers in the imperial guard regiments, garrisoned in St Petersburg.*


All of that, a reasonably educated Russian does know

What he does not know is that Pushkin’s poem, of course, is based on a translation from the French. The original lines, which Alexander Pushkin translated, and adapted to Russian had been written decades ago, by a French philosophe Denis Diderot

> La Nature n’a fait ni serviteur ni maître;
> Je ne veux ni donner, ni recevoir de lois.
> Et ses mains ourdiraient les entrailles du prêtre,
> Au défaut d’un cordon pour étrangler les rois
> 
> (Denis Diderot, 1772)

Literally,

> The Nature knows neither servant, nor master;
> I don’t want to give, nor to receive laws.
> And the hands would disembowel the priest,
> To make a rope for strangling the king.

And, yes, this quote, too, gives a lot for understanding of how the things work in this world, practically speaking. Because when I say that Diderot was a philosophe, I don’t mean that he was an ‘academic philosopher’ in modern sense or anything like that. In the 18th c France, the word les philosophes, referred to the public intellectuals, to the people focused on changing and shaping the public opinion. The opinion makers.

And Diderot’s story shows the exact limits of what the podcasters, like himself, can and cannot do.

What they cannot do, is to command politics. They can’t tell the king to do because he won’t listen

What they can do, is to drop poison in people’s mind. Drop by a drop, bit by a bit.

Over the long run, their poison becomes the established fact, the ideological and intellectual landscape everyone, including the kings and generals, will be operating within. They will shape the world, will terraform it. Their words will turn into the seas, and the mountains of the new world.

Except - it won’t be happening in their lifetime.

Notice how everything Diderot, and other *les philosophes* had been preaching, did indeed happen

Except Diderot never saw any of it. By that point, he had been long dead

But his ideas, and his poison, kept working, and worked decades, and generations after his death, worked all around the globe, including the far of St Petersburg. I sometimes think that the major reason the French revolution seems to be a little bit less relevant today, being seen as some kind of ancient history, is that the French revolution has won so completely, and won everywhere, no exceptions.

There is simply nothing more to debate, or to polarise about.

Now that gives some additional context, to the might makes right paradigm. And that context is: propaganda matters. We all know the famous ‘power grows out of the barrel of a gun’ quote from Mao Zedong. But that quote, too, is a piece of propaganda. In reality, during the Long March, his troops abandoned all of their artillery, even the small calibre mortars, because they were just too heavy to carry over the mountains.

But they never abandoned the printing press. Because again, propaganda matters.

Now let’s return to Ronald Syme, and set the blueprint of our studies. What do we need to do, practically speaking.

What we need to do is:

Step one. Buy yourself a physical copy of the book. There is a very affordable softcover version, but - if you can afford it - I would go for a hardcover version. I would, however, advice against the ebook (unless, of course, you are facing some very severe budget constraints). It’s not the same.

First, 90% of us are already staring at screen way too much every day. We need to organise ourselves some activities where we won’t be staring at screen and interacting with something physical. Physical book counts.

Second, physical book makes it much easier to take notes. If you have never annotated [= written & drawn in] a book before, you must learn this now, and the book of Syme, specifically, is a great starting place to learn this art

Content of the course. There are thirty-three chapters in this book, including conclusion & introduction. Read one chapter every day. No more, no less.

There is no need to do less, because the chapters are so short. The longest is just 21 page long. The shortest 9 page long. The rest range somewhere in between. That is what a working person can realistically finish, in one day, reading slowly.

No need to do more, because chapters are so dense. Extremely, extremely dense & contain a lot of information + judgements. I find that reading one chapter per day is just enough and is exactly what I can digest, so I would recommend the same to you.

This is the place to start learning the art of slow pensive reading. We are not in a rush, and are not hurrying anywhere. Our goal is to spend some time on a good book, which means - in a company of an interesting person. Reading his thoughts, slowly & attentively, contemplating on them, that’s what feeds our brain, and cultivates our own consciousness.

What I like about Syme’s book is that it is so perfectly measured & structured for an individual contemplation. So, it gives you a perfect material for a self-taught course, that does not really require any external instructor, but yourself.

One more additional perk of this book is that it is a great place to start taking notes, straight in the book. If you never did it, it’s time to learn now. The book of Syme is rich & complex enough that you need to take notes, just to navigate through the material. But - at the same time - it’s coherent, well-organised and laconic enough, so that it will be easy for you to annotate. That makes it a good starting point.

We are not saying goodbye to Ronald Syme. I will be returning to his work again, and again. But for today, it will suffice. If you buy a book, and start doing your own reading, our journey will make a more interesting and a more rewarding experience.
