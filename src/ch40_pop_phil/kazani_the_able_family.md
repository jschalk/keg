> On the Social Origins of Vladimir Lenin.

<div align="center"><img src="images/old_family_portrait.webp" alt="Multiple generations family photo" width="300"></div>

Disraeli instructed us to read no history but biography[^1]. I would modestly propose an amendment to this maxim. Read no history but **family biography.**

Individual life is short. There is not much you can do[^2]. To accomplish something big, you need to accumulate incremental achievements over a few generations. Step by step, fathers build the foundation for their sons to stand upon. It is for the sons to play big. But their chance to play is based on the ancestral sacrifice.

Contrary to the widespread opinion, the meritocratic principle does, indeed, work in practice. It is, however, best visible in the long run, over the lives of several generations. And that is because it is the **family rather than an individual that makes for a basic unit of social mobility.**

As Joseph Schumpeter pointed out:

<div align="center">

### *‘‘Not only does modern capitalism proffer means to shelter and nurse almost any kind of ability … but by the very law of its structure it tends to send up the able individual and, much more effectively, **the able family**’’

(Joseph Schumpeter. Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy)*
</div>

Not so much an able individual, as an able family will be sent up.

And, perhaps, no family can illustrate this principle better than the family of Vladimir Lenin. His grandfather was a fugitive serf who made himself a free townsman. His father was a townsman who made himself a nobleman. And he was nobleman who made himself a dictator. From serfs to townsmen, from townsmen to noblemen, from noblemen to dictators, all in three generations.

That was, beyond any doubt, an extraordinary family; a brilliant example of meritocracy, of upward social mobility, and of the socio-political effects they produce.

**Grandfather**

Nikolay Ulyanov was born in 1768[^3], as a serf.

And when I say “serf”, I mean a “field slave”.

In the late 18th c., serfs made for over 90% of the Russian Empire’s population. Most were villagers, living in the countryside and working on the aristocratic estates. Serfs had little legal protection or personal autonomy. They were bought and sold, mortgaged and auctioned. They could be sold by the entire villages, they could be sold individually, they could be sold far away, separated from their family and their kin.

It didn’t become like that overnight. In fact, the state and the status of peasantry worsened progressively, for generations[^4], until it finally hit the bottom. The reign of Catherine II (1762-1796) was the darkest age of peasantry, the lowest point in the history of the Russian commonfolk. By that time, it was the chattel slavery, in all but name.

And Nikolay Ulyanov had a misfortune to be born in the age of Catherine.

“Bargaining” by Nikolay Nevrev. This painting is supposed to present the 19th c. realities.

There was, however, a nuance. Serfs had no rights, and could be all used as field slaves. And yet, the plantation mode of economy could be more profitable or less profitable depending on the **soil fertility**.

To understand the history, you need to understand geography. And to understand the Russian geography, you need to internalise one crucial dichotomy:

<div align="center"><img src="images/black_soils.webp" alt="Blacks Soils vs Non Black Soils" width="700"></div>

**Blacks Soils vs Non Black Soils**. Which means the Fertile South vs Infertile North.

The south of what is now European Russia is classified as the “Black Soils” area. Historically, a part of the Eurasian Steppe, it was highly fertile producing amazing crop yields. This area ranged from the good to great for grain farming.

To the north, you have a cold, swampy, foresty, “Non Black Soils” country. With rare exceptions, it it is just infertile. Harvests are poor, yields are low[^5]. In terms of grain farming, it ranges from bad to absolutely awful.

<div align="center"><img src="images/androsovo_map.webp" alt="Ulyanov’s birthplace. Androsovo village in the infertile Nizhny Novgorod region." width="700"></div>

Ulyanov’s birthplace. Androsovo village in the infertile Nizhny Novgorod region.


So, what does it mean in practice?

Soil fertility had been a major constraint on the productivity of grain farming and, therefore, on the profitability of noble estates. Consequently, the institution of serfdom produced very different economic outcomes depending on the fertility of soil.

When the soils were fertile, nobility focused on the grain farming and employed all of their serfs in the grain farming full-time. Consequently, the Black Soils region produced the least free serfs in all of Russia. As the plantation mode of economy what profitable, what you had was many and many grain plantations in the New World style.

And vice versa, the infertility of Non-Black soils produced relatively freer peasantry. As the grain farming was not particularly profitable, there were hardly any New World style plantations in the area. Plantation model of business just did not generate too much revenue.

Serfdom + Fertile Soil = Profitable Plantations → Field slavery

Serfdom + Infertile Soil = Unprofitable Plantations → Freer peasantry

When you can’t run a profitable plantation, you have to generate the cashflow otherwise and to milk your peasants otherwise. One solution could be: let them do handicrafts and sell their product on the market. Once they collect revenue, the must share it with you. That’s how the infertile north produced so much rural industry.

Another alternative was: let them learn trades and become itinerant tradesmen. Once they return to your estate - and they must return to renew their papers - they must pay you, as their master and owner.

Of course, the latter option includes a certain element of risk.

A serf must return to his master to renew the papers. But there is always a chance that he won’t. Then he becomes an illegal, an undocumented, a highly problematic status in the overbureaucratised Russian Empire. Not much different from an outlaw. Many would shun this fate.

Many, but not Lenin’s grandfather.

Nikolay Ulyanov was a serf who learnt the art of sewing. In 1791, his master was foolish enough to let him work as an itinerant tailor. Nikolay was soon to return, to renew his papers, as he had to. He never did.

He escaped to Astrakhan instead.

<div align="center"><img src="images/astrakhan_map.webp" alt="Astrakhan on the confluence of Volga river and the Caspian Sea" width="700"></div>

*Astrakhan on the confluence of Volga river and the Caspian Sea*

Astrakhan was a seaport, located in the mouth of Volga where it meets the Caspian sea. Historically, it had been the Russian window to the East, to the Central Asian and the Iranic world. Far off, distant and isolated from the capital, it was a major melting pot with all sorts of people from all sorts of nations messing around. Caravans were coming from the steppes, ships were coming from the sea.

<div align="center"><img src="images/astrakhan_drawing.webp" alt="Astrakhan in the mid 18th c." width="700"></div>

*Astrakhan in the mid 18th c.*

It was naturally a place, where it was easy to get lost. Perhaps even more importantly, it was a place where it was easy to legalise.

Far off from the population centres of Russian Empire, Astrakhan suffered from the lack of manpower. Local authorities needed the labor for all sorts of vocations. They wanted to populate and expand the agglomeration, settling the Lower Volga steppe. The problem, however, was that pretty much all of the manpower available was either foreigners or runaway serfs flocking there from the upstream country[^6].

This created a conflict of interest between the noblemen of Central Russian who wanted their serfs back and the Astrakhan regional government who wanted more people on the ground. Landowners lobbied for the hardline approach, regional government wanted a softer, more liberal policy towards the fugitives.

As long as Catherine lived, this conflict of interest was almost invariably resolved in the favour of landowners. Fugitives were to be beaten with a knout thrice to make them disclose their origins and name their owner. In practical terms, that meant that the legalisation was improbable, if not impossible as very few could stand through the torture.

In 1796, Catherine dies and the new emperor Paul makes an immediate U-turn on many and many of her policies, including the treatment of the fugitives. In 1797, he concedes to the pleas of the Astrakhan authorities and proclaims a general amnesty of the fugitive serfs, residing there. Allowing them to legalise and get an official free status that opened a road for the upward social mobility for many and many.

Such as Nikolay Ulyanov.

<div align="center"><img src="images/catherine2_paul1.webp" alt="Two paintings. One of Catherine II, and one Paul I" width="700"></div>

*Catherine II Paul I*

By 1799, Ulyanov had been official stripped from his serf status and became a state owned peasant. Which meant a free peasant basically, as he did not have an owner anymore. Hence, he went up and up only. In a few years, he became a townsman[^7] officially registered in a village near Astrakhan. Later, he managed to re-register in Astrakhan, getting an official hukou in the city.

Finally, he was admitted into the tradesman guild[^8].

That is a meteoric rise, incredible rise. Which became possible thanks to a major U-turn in policy with the death of Catherine II.

> Ding-dong! The Witch is dead. Which old Witch? The Wicked Witch! Ding-dong! The Wicked Witch is dead.

*If Catherine II enjoys positive reputation, that is because history is written by the aristocracy.*
