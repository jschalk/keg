**Author:** Emmanuel Schalk

## Down in the West Texas Town of El Paso

I grew up in El Paso, went to UTEP and became friends with the Physics people. They told me that a Nobel Prize recipient was coming to El Paso and was going to give a talk. I went to go see him and he said he liked the Marty Robins song "Rosa's Cantina" and wanted to go to Rosa's Cantina himself. Rosa's Cantina is still around, I've eaten there once and had a very very good chile relleno. I do recommend. 

Anyway, this guy, probably [Dr. Anthony Leggett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Leggett), as a Nobel Prize guy has a special status in the world and at least some people want to hear him talk. So he gives talks. It's part of the responsiblities of being a Nobel guy. And here was the dramatic thin I took away from his presentation 

<div align="center">

#### He was terrible and he didn't care.
</div>

He had a slide desk and he went through the slides and talked during each one. Individually they were interesting slides but put together there was a confusing mess. It really felt like he made the presentation 3 weeks after getting the Nobel and had never updated it in the years since. Never improving it. And there were interesting nuggets to work with. 

In one slide he showed a scan of old wrinkled graph paper with the crazy data that indicated that at super low temperature hydrogen liquid no longer has friction. That's what I understood, I'm sure I got that wrong. Later he told a fun little story about how he got these results at 2am while working obsessively on his research. He claimed that the only reason he was able to be so obsessive is that he refused distractions including his wife's request that he learn her native language. 

*We love each other so much. Can you learn my parents' language so that you can join the family more fully.* 

***No.** I have things to do.*

Pretty funny. And he was right. 

But even this anecdote couldn't save the presentation. Instead of learning to love science or learning or believing in your dreams the theme of the night was 'I don't have to try because I won the Nobel'. My Jeffery from Mexico City then explained to me that this is exactly what happens. The "Curse of the Nobel" happens to scientists and thinkers when they win: they become idiots, coasting on their past achievements. Jeffery said that when this Nobel prize in Physics guy spoke to the Physics department, an audience trained and ready for an advanced lecture of hyperfluidity, he gave the exact same lecture. Just a complete waste of the physicists' time.

Nobel Prize guy probably gave 50 lectures a year. If he had just put in 10 minutes before his lecture to improve just one thing at a time the presentations would have been much better. 

But he didn't. And kindly provided for me an example of how not to do it. 


## Being good at writing

Being good at writing is difficult. Many an successful author has discussed how much they practiced before they were good. Good for them. However I will never get as good as them no matter how much I practice. They have work ethic and talent. Here's what I will do. 

<div align="center">

#### I will accept where I'm starting from.
</div>

I know my writing is not that the level of the authors I admire. I will never get there. So what am I going to do about it? Quit?

Well actually that's exactly what I've been doing about it. I've continuously quit writing because I don't like my sentences or format or final result. But then I unable to quit for more then I few hours because I'm passionate.

I need to produce and instead of quitting I need to accept that my writing will never reach the heights I want and but my audience will notice if I try. I will continue trying. Putting in the work. This sounds like cliches.

<div align="center">

#### Just cause it's a cliche doesn't mean it's wrong. 
</div>

So here I go. Leaping in.
